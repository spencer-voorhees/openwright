The Helios Survey

How a deep-space probe charts an uncharted star system in a single orbital pass, what that pass found, and what it deliberately left for the next cycle.

Executive summary

The Helios probe reached Veridia with fuel for a single transit. In one continuous sweep from the inner orbit to the outer dark it returned a fused, survey-grade map of the inner system, a partial read of the debris belt, and a low-resolution gravimetric sketch of everything beyond. The headline is not the coverage number. It is that the survey is honest about exactly where its confidence ends.

Key finding. Two candidate bodies in the mid belt show spectral signatures consistent with water ice. They are candidates, not confirmations — the belt was read at medium resolution on a single pass.

The approach

There was no second approach, no loiter, and no return burn to re-image a shell we rushed. That constraint shaped every decision below it.

Why a single pass

Repeated sweeps hide their gaps in the overlap; coverage looks high while confidence quietly is not. One clean pass trades raw coverage for a single coordinate frame and a clear, honest boundary where resolution falls off. The reasons, in order of weight:

  • One coordinate frame across the whole system — no seams to reconcile later.
  • No fuel or schedule spent on return burns that mostly re-image known ground.
  • A boundary the next cycle can target precisely instead of guessing at.

Method

The pass followed a fixed procedure. The order matters — each step depends on the one before it.

  1. Fix the probe's position against two reference stars.
  2. Open every sensor array and hold a constant burn through the system.
  3. Let the map assemble itself from the overlap, shell by shell.
  4. Re-fix position before any maneuver call, to bound accumulated drift.
A survey flown in one pass is honest about its blind spots. One stitched together from many is only ever as honest as its stitching.

The instruments

Three sensor arrays carried the survey, each tuned to a different shell and a different question. Where their ranges overlap, the fused map is strongest; where only one reads, it is exactly as strong as that one array.

Coverage

Coverage is reported per shell, not as a single system figure, because a single number would average away precisely the distinction that matters: the inner orbit is a survey, the outer dark is a sketch.

ShellCoverageGrade
Inner orbit96%Survey

Read with care. Position error grows the further the probe travels from its reference stars. Outer-dark coordinates carry the widest uncertainty and should be treated as approximate.

Findings

The inner orbit returned cleanly and needs no caveats. The interesting, and more delicate, results are further out.

  • Four inner planets resolved at survey grade; orbits and masses are firm.
  • The debris belt is denser than modeled, with two ice candidates noted above.
  • A gravimetric anomaly in the outer dark suggests an unseen body; resolution is too low to call it.

The anomaly is logged in the telemetry under a provisional tag, pending a higher-resolution pass:

OBJ-OD-0007   grav_anomaly   conf=low   shell=outer_dark
              dr=0.04 AU      grade=sketch   action=defer

Risks and limits

Do not act on the outer shell alone. No follow-up burn should be tasked off outer-dark data by itself — it has not cleared survey grade, and a maneuver planned against a sketch can miss by more than the sketch can see.

Everything the survey is unsure of, it says so plainly and in place. That is the discipline a single pass buys: the map and its blind spots arrive together, in the same frame, so no one downstream mistakes a sketch for a survey.

Recommendations

  1. Approve a targeted cycle 13 pass over the outer dark to resolve OBJ-OD-0007.
  2. Release the inner-orbit survey to the cartography group now — it is final.
  3. Hold the belt ice candidates as provisional until a medium-or-better re-read.
