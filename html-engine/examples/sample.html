Deep Field Brief · Cycle 12

The Helios Survey

Charting an uncharted star system in a single orbital pass — and what that one pass teaches about working under hard constraints.

Survey Directorate · Prepared for the Standing Committee Confidential draft
The mission

One probe. One window. One pass.

The Helios probe reaches the Veridia system with fuel for a single transit. There is no second approach, no loiter, no return burn to re-image a shell we rushed. Every instrument has to earn its place in one continuous sweep from the inner orbit to the outer dark.

That constraint is not a limitation to apologize for. It is the design. A survey built to be flown once is forced to be honest about what it can and cannot see — and that honesty is the most valuable thing it brings home.

The approach

The claim

A survey flown in one pass is honest about its blind spots.

Repeated sweeps hide their gaps in the overlap. One pass shows exactly where the signal ends — the single most useful line on the chart.

Two ways to fly it

Why not just go back for more?

Repeated sweepsRejected

Many partial passes

Each return burn costs fuel and time, and the seams between passes hide exactly the gaps you most need to find. Coverage looks high; confidence is not.

Single passChosen

One continuous sweep

A single coordinate frame across the whole system, no stitching, and a clear honest boundary where resolution falls off. Less coverage, far more trust.

What to watch

Honest about the gaps

By design. The outer dark is a gravimetric sketch, not a survey. Treat its features as candidates, not findings.
Drift. Position error accumulates the further the probe travels from its two reference stars. Re-fix before any maneuver call.
Do not. Do not task a follow-up burn off the outer-shell data alone. It has not cleared survey grade.
